Scene safe. Ambient temperature is 52°F with a breeze — a 6-year-old in shock loses heat fast, and hypothermia is the 'H' in MARCH. Keep him covered from the first minute. Gravel access road will slow the departure; back the rig in.

Initial Contact
A 6-year-old, 20 kg, ten-foot fall onto his left side, and his blood pressure is 98/58 — which is NORMAL for his age. That number is the trap, and it will kill him if the crew believes it. MATC's pediatric hypotension threshold for a 6-year-old is 70 + (2 × 6) = 82 mmHg systolic. He is above it, and he is in profound shock anyway. Children are compensatory masters: they clamp down peripherally and run their heart rate up, holding a normal pressure until they have lost a devastating fraction of their blood volume, and then they do not fade — they fall off a cliff. MATC states this outright in the trauma guideline's safety considerations: patients with compensated shock may not manifest hypotension until severe blood loss has occurred. Everything else on this child is screaming. Heart rate 148. Capillary refill 4 seconds. Pale, cool skin. Left upper quadrant guarding after a direct left-side impact — a splenic injury until proven otherwise. And the silence. A 6-year-old who falls ten feet and does not cry is not brave and is not stoic; he is not perfusing his brain. The protocol answer is not complicated and it is not slow: MARCH, 20 mL/kg of crystalloid, scene time under ten minutes, and drive to the trauma center.

Expected Actions
critical
MARCH primary survey: Massive hemorrhage, Airway, Respiratory, Circulation, Head/Hypothermia
Primary survey (Use "MARCH" algorithm): Massive Hemorrhage: Initial visual and body sweep to assess for penetrating wounds and severe life-threatening hemorrhage. Airway: Assess airway patency by asking the patient basic questions to assess for stridor and ease of air movement; Evaluate mental status for ability to protect airway. Respiratory/Breathing: Assess respiratory rate and pattern; Assess for tracheal deviation; Assess symmetry of chest wall movement; Listen bilaterally on lateral chest wall for breath sounds. Circulation: Assess blood pressure and heart rate. Head injury/Hypothermia: Perform initial neurologic status assessment of GCS/AVPU and pupillary size and responsiveness; Assess for gross motor movement of extremities; Prevent hypothermia.

Load and go; bolus en route
Trigger: Scene time under 10 minutes. Child packaged with spinal motion restriction, warmed, oxygenated, and transported. 400 mL crystalloid bolus running en route with reassessment.
Packaged and rolling in eight minutes. The 400 mL bolus is running through a line established in the back of a moving ambulance, because the protocol says explicitly not to delay transport to obtain IV access — and if the line had been difficult, the answer was an IO, not a longer scene time. He responds the way a compensating child responds: the heart rate comes down from 148 to 132, capillary refill improves to 3 seconds, and he mumbles a one-word answer when asked his name. Crystalloid replaces volume; it does not replace blood, and it does not close a lacerated spleen. The destination decision is the one that matters now, and MATC does not leave it to taste: transport to the highest level of care within the trauma system, per the ACS-COT field triage guideline. The community hospital five minutes away has no pediatric surgeon. Twenty minutes to a Level 1 pediatric trauma center is the correct answer even though it is the longer drive, because the thing this child needs is an operating room, and only one of those two buildings has the right one.

Improper Care Branch
Stayed on scene; reassured by normal BP
Trigger: Crew remained on scene past 10 minutes, reassured by a blood pressure that was 'normal for age.' The child exhausted his compensatory reserve and decompensated abruptly into uncompensated hemorrhagic shock.
The crew did a thorough job on that playground. A complete head-to-toe. Two careful IV attempts in a child with no visible veins. A splint for a wrist that was never broken. A conversation about whether to wait for the parents. Twelve minutes in, the blood pressure was still 'normal,' and that was the number they kept coming back to. At the ten-minute mark he stopped compensating. Children do not decline gradually — they hold the line, and hold it, and hold it, and then the line breaks all at once. His pressure fell from 98 to 66 systolic in under two minutes. Radial pulses are gone. His skin is mottled. His heart rate spiked to 172 and has now begun to FALL toward 44, which in a bleeding child is not a rhythm problem — it is the agonal bradycardia of a heart with nothing left to pump. He is minutes from a traumatic arrest on a playground twenty minutes from the only surgeon who could have saved him. The lesson is exactly the one the protocol wrote down: patients with compensated shock may not manifest hypotension until severe blood loss has occurred. The blood pressure was never a measure of how he was doing. It was a measure of how long he could keep pretending.
