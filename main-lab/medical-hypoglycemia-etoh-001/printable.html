Initial Contact
A 42-year-old diabetic man who reeks of beer, slurs, sweats, and swings at anyone who touches him. Every surface cue on this scene says 'drunk,' and a dismissive spouse is standing in the doorway confirming it for free. He is hypoglycemic at 42 mg/dL, and the ONLY way the crew finds that out is by putting a glucometer on him. This is not a subtle protocol point — MATC's Hypoglycemia guideline explicitly lists 'patients with suspected alcohol ingestion' and 'patients who appear to be intoxicated' as INCLUSION criteria. The smell of alcohol is a reason to check a sugar, not a reason to skip one. The cool, diaphoretic skin is the physical tell that does not belong to intoxication. His combativeness is not belligerence — MATC states plainly that hypoglycemic patients can be irritable and violent. And the wife's refusal is legally irrelevant: this patient has impaired decision-making capacity, which is the entry criterion for the Altered Mental Status guideline, and an altered patient cannot refuse care through a proxy who is not his guardian.

Improper Care Branch
IV failed — Glucagon given
Trigger: Hypoglycemia identified at 42 mg/dL, but IV access cannot be established — the patient is combative, diaphoretic, and peripherally shut down, and attempts have failed. Glucagon is now the protocol-authorized option.
This is a CORRECT path, not an error — and it is the one place MATC authorizes glucagon. The patient is thrashing and soaked in sweat, his veins are flat, and two IV attempts have failed. MATC lists glucagon IM/IN explicitly as 'an option for patients for whom IV access cannot be established' — a fallback, not a first choice. Adult dose is 1 mg IM/IN. The crew must understand what they have bought: glucagon does not deliver glucose, it tells the liver to release stored glycogen. Onset is slow (10–15 minutes) compared to IV dextrose, and this patient has been drinking heavily on an empty stomach for hours — the population in whom hepatic glycogen stores are most likely to be depleted. So the crew gives the glucagon AND keeps working the access problem, because if he does not wake up, the answer is a line and dextrose, not a second dose of hope. The protocol's own fallback ladder ends with disabling an insulin pump if the above cannot be completed — not applicable here, since he is on injected glargine.

The lesson is not subtle and it is not really about diabetes — it is about what happens when a crew lets a plausible story do the work of an assessment.
