Initial Contact
An 88-year-old man is pulseless and apneic in a hospital bed, and standing between the crew and the patient is a calm, sincere, grieving son saying the words that make this a summative: "he has a DNR." Nobody on this scene is lying — the family is probably right that a document exists somewhere — but MATC's DNR/advance-directives protocol is unambiguous about what counts: the document (or a state-sanctioned identification bracelet) must be IMMEDIATELY AVAILABLE, and it is only valid if it conforms to state specifications, is intact, and displays the patient's name. A verbal report of a DNR in a safe deposit box meets none of that. The protocol also names the crew's move when validity is in question: proceed with the resuscitation until additional information clarifies the best course of action — and its Patient Safety Consideration says it even more plainly: when the appropriateness of withholding resuscitation is questioned, initiate CPR immediately and contact medical direction. Meanwhile the cardiac arrest protocol demands the assessment stay focused — just enough to reveal pulselessness — with all further history gathered from the family WHILE treatment is ongoing. One more protocol nuance the source material glosses over: MATC recognizes that, in the absence of written directions, a person holding healthcare power of attorney may prescribe limits of treatment as specified from state to state — so the crew should ask; here, no one on scene has POA documentation either. The crew that passes this scenario confirms the arrest in seconds, asks one clear question about paperwork, explains their obligation with empathy, and puts hands on the chest.

Engine crew delegated
Trigger: Engine 4 arrives immediately after compressions are underway and pads are placed. "Engine 4 is here." Four more sets of hands just walked into a small bedroom, and what happens next determines whether this becomes a high-performance resuscitation or a crowd. MATC's answer is the pit-crew model: two rescuers on opposite sides of the chest alternating compressions every 100 to avoid fatigue, dedicated hands on two-person BVM ventilation, a crew member supporting the family, and one clinician — the code leader — stepping back to oversee the entire response rather than performing tasks. The protocol also tells the crew what NOT to do yet: avoid advanced airway placement during the first four cycles (~10 minutes) — the OPA/BVM strategy continues — and do not package or move the patient, because compressions during movement lose depth, rate, and recoil; the resuscitation happens right here in the bedroom. The AED advises no shock at every 2-minute analysis. With the resuscitation now running itself, the DNR question is still open — and the code leader's next move is the one the protocol names explicitly: contact medical direction. Delegation is what buys the code leader the freedom to make that call.

Improper Care Branch
CPR withheld on verbal DNR
Trigger: The crew withheld CPR based solely on the family's verbal statement that a DNR exists. No physical order, bracelet, or POA documentation was ever produced or examined. The crew stands by while no resuscitation occurs and downtime accumulates.
The crew took the son's word for it. No compressions, no pads, no ventilations — two providers standing in a quiet bedroom while a potentially resuscitatable patient accumulates downtime, waiting for a document that is in a safe deposit box across town. This is the critical failure of the summative. The MATC DNR protocol never authorizes this: its entry requirement is a document or bracelet IMMEDIATELY AVAILABLE, its validity test requires an intact, state-conforming order displaying the patient's name, and its instruction for any case where validity is in question is to proceed with resuscitation. The cardiac arrest protocol's exclusion applies only to an IDENTIFIABLE DNR order — and nothing has been identified, only described. Every minute that passes unrepaired makes the physiologic hole deeper: EtCO2 will start lower, and the 20-minute termination clock will eventually be reached without the patient ever having received the resuscitation the protocol owed him — every element of it, compressions, AED analysis, and ventilations, currently owed and undelivered. The recovery move is available at any moment and is written verbatim in the protocol's Patient Safety Considerations: initiate CPR immediately and contact medical direction. The instructor should let the discomfort build — the silence in the room IS the lesson — and reward the student who breaks it by moving to the chest.
