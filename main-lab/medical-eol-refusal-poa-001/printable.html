Scene
Dispatch
Dispatch: 84-year-old male, difficulty breathing at a private residence. Caller is the patient's adult son, states his father is 'refusing to help himself.' Caller reports he holds power of attorney for the patient and is requesting transport. Caller sounds agitated.
Location
Living room of a small, tidy-but-crowded single-story home. The room is cluttered with medical supplies: an oxygen concentrator humming in the corner with tubing snaking across the carpet, a nebulizer machine and masks on the side table, pill organizers, and a walker beside the recliner where the patient sits.
Time
2:30 PM, weekday afternoon
Safety
No physical threats on arrival. Oxygen concentrator in use — no smoking/ignition sources. Oxygen tubing across the floor is a trip hazard. The primary scene-management problem is interpersonal: the son becomes progressively more agitated and confrontational as the encounter proceeds. MATC's end-of-life guidance is explicit that scene safety should be considered when deciding on management — position the crew so no one is between escalating family and the exit, and de-escalate early.
Bystanders
Greg Brandt, the patient's son (mid-50s), is on scene. He is anxious, pacing, and holding a manila folder of paperwork. He genuinely loves his father and is frightened — but he is convinced that his Power of Attorney paperwork makes him 'the decision-maker' and that the crew works for him. He will escalate from worried to demanding to shouting if not managed with empathy and clear education.

Initial Contact
An 84-year-old man with end-stage CHF and COPD sits comfortably in his recliner on his home 2L oxygen, mildly dyspneic, wearing a valid DNR bracelet, and telling the crew — kindly, clearly, in full sentences — that he is not going to the hospital. Nothing about this scene is a resuscitation problem: he has a pulse, he is breathing, he is alert. MATC's DNR/advance-directives protocol says exactly what to do with that combination: verify the bracelet is a valid exclusion to resuscitation, and then provide standard appropriate treatment under existing protocols according to his condition — a DNR is not 'do not treat.' The respiratory distress protocol supplies the standard treatment at this crew's scope: full vitals with temperature and pulse oximetry, a respiratory history that captures his home O2 and nebulizer use and prior admissions, breath sounds to differentiate fluid-overload from bronchospastic etiology, and oxygen titrated to a 94–98% target (he is at 94% on his home 2L — at target). The end-of-life protocol adds the frame the whole scenario turns on: because this patient can communicate and has the capacity to make decisions, the crew consults directly with HIM about treatment and transport — not with the folder of paperwork his son is carrying. Everything he consents to, he gets. Nothing he refuses can be forced.
