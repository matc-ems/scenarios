A single dose of IM epinephrine has a short duration of effect, the mast-cell mediator release is not finished, and the stinger delivered a full venom load. Crews routinely stand down here — they slow the transport decision, break down the airway kit, and stop reassessing. The crew that packages and moves during this window is the crew that has an airway and a working line when the patient crashes.

The antihistamine did exactly what antihistamines do — it took some of the itch away while the patient decompensated. The rescue is not complicated: give the epinephrine that was owed ten minutes ago, and give it now.

Improper Care Branch
Second Epi withheld
Trigger: The second IM epinephrine dose was never given. Upper-airway edema progressed to complete obstruction; stridor ceased (no air movement), the patient became profoundly hypoxic, then bradycardic, and arrested in pulseless electrical activity.
The crew recognized the deterioration but never repeated the epinephrine — fixated on the airway bag, or unwilling to redose a patient already tachycardic at 138, or waiting out the far end of the 5–15 minute window. The airway did not wait. Edema progressed to complete upper-airway obstruction; the patient became silent (no stridor, because no air moves), hypoxic, then bradycardic, and arrested in PEA. This is the scenario's worst outcome and its sharpest lesson: a heart rate of 138 in anaphylaxis is not a contraindication to epinephrine — it is the shock state that epinephrine treats. Resuscitation here is not a standard medical code. This is an arrest of RESPIRATORY etiology, so the airway comes early and passive NRB oxygenation is explicitly not indicated. And the airway that caused the arrest is the same airway that will defeat intubation: when the crew cannot ventilate and cannot intubate through an obstructed glottis, the protocol's answer is a surgical airway.

Expected Actions
critical
High-quality compressions: 100–120/min, 2–2.4 in depth, full recoil, minimal interruption
Effective chest compressions and defibrillation are the most important therapies to the patient in cardiac arrest. Effective chest compressions are defined as: A rate of greater than 100 and less than 120 compressions/minute; Depth of at least 2 inches (5 cm) and less than 2.4 inches (6 cm) for adults and children; Allow for complete chest recoil (avoid leaning); Minimize interruptions in compressions; Avoid rescuer fatigue by rotating rescuers at least every 2 minutes.

important
EtCO2 to gauge compression quality — under 10 mmHg means improve compressions
Quantitative end-tidal capnography (EtCO2) should be used to monitor effectiveness of chest compressions. If EtCO2 less than 10 mmHg during the initial phases of resuscitation, attempt to improve chest compression quality. Is strongly encouraged in patients in cardiac arrest: to monitor quality of CPR; as an early indicator of ROSC (rapid increase of 10–15 in EtCO2); to assist in evaluating prognosis for survival.

important
Resuscitate on scene — compressions in a moving ambulance are less effective and unsafe
Patient Safety Considerations: Performing manual chest compressions in a moving vehicle may pose a clinician safety concern. In addition, manual chest compressions during patient movement are less effective in regard to hands on time, depth, recoil and rate. Ideally, patients should be resuscitated as close to the scene as operationally possible. Risks and benefits should be considered before patient movement in cardiac arrest situations.
