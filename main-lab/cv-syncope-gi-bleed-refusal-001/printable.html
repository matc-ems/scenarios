Initial Contact
This is the most dangerous patient a crew will meet all shift, and she is going to smile at them and ask them to leave. She is A&O×4, her vitals look unremarkable, and she has a perfectly plausible story — she stood up too fast during a hymn. Every instinct says vasovagal, sign the refusal, clear the call. Here is what MATC actually says. The Syncope guideline's Review of Systems has a line item for OCCULT BLOOD LOSS (GI/GU), and its list of high-risk causes of syncope names GI BLEED explicitly under hemorrhagic. Its Additional Evaluation is not optional: cardiac monitoring, SpO2, ongoing vitals, 12-lead EKG, and a blood glucose. And its Key Consideration 2 is the sentence that governs this whole call — decisions about delayed or non-transport are for patients where a LOWER-RISK etiology is suspected, and they are made in consultation with medical direction. Nothing about a 72-year-old anticoagulated woman who syncopes is low-risk. The crew's job in this phase is to do the workup the protocol demands and to ask the questions that make her stop being reassuring — because she will not offer the melena. Someone has to ask.

A black, tarry, foul-smelling stool this morning that she rationalized as iron pills she does not take. And ten days of daily ibuprofen layered on top of warfarin — an NSAID stripping her gastric mucosa while an anticoagulant guarantees that whatever it opens will not close. This is an upper GI hemorrhage in a fully anticoagulated 72-year-old, and MATC's Abdominal Pain guideline flags it in one sentence: abdominal pain in older adults, patients with bleeding disorders, and patients on anticoagulation medications may be a harbinger for severe illness. Her repeat pressure has slipped and her pulse has climbed as far as a beta-blocked heart can take it. AND SHE STILL SAYS NO. She is competent, she understands the words, and she is still going to walk to her car. This is where the protocol stops being about medicine and becomes about disposition: MATC's Syncope guideline authorizes delayed or non-transport ONLY where a LOWER-RISK etiology is suspected, and ONLY in consultation with medical direction. Improper Care Branch
Refusal accepted; no workup, no med control
Trigger: Refusal accepted without the required syncope workup, without asking about occult blood loss, and without consulting medical direction. The patient syncopized again in the parking lot and struck her head while fully anticoagulated.
The crew took the easy story. No 12-lead, no monitor, no question about her stool, no call to medical direction — just a signature on a refusal form and a well-appearing 72-year-old walking out to her car. She made it about forty feet across the parking lot before her second syncopal episode, and this time there was no pew to catch her. She struck the back of her head on the asphalt. She is now a fully anticoagulated patient with a head injury and an ongoing GI hemorrhage, which is roughly the worst combination in geriatric prehospital medicine: warfarin means an intracranial bleed does not tamponade, and the GI bleed means she has no reserve to lose. She is GCS 12 and dropping, hypotensive at 82/48, with a boggy occipital hematoma. Everything the crew skipped is now mandatory, and it is being done in a parking lot with a worse patient. Note the honest part of this failure: she had capacity. She could have refused even a perfect crew. But a perfect crew would have done the workup, found the melena, called a physician, and documented all of it — and the protocol says that is what non-transport requires.
