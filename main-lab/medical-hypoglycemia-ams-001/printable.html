Glucagon given
Trigger: Blood glucose 45 mg/dL identified, oral glucose correctly withheld, and glucagon 1 mg IM/IN administered under the protocol's no-IV-access clause. Glucagon mobilizes hepatic glycogen, so the response is slower than IV dextrose: over ~15 minutes mental status returns to Alert and Oriented ×4; repeat glucose 110 mg/dL.
Glucagon works the way its mechanism promises — slowly. It does not deliver glucose; it asks the liver to release stored glycogen, so the first minutes after the injection change nothing, and the airway watch continues: a hypoglycemic patient who has not yet turned the corner can still lose his airway, and oral glucose remains contraindicated until he can follow commands. Then the mumbling organizes into words; he begins looking at the providers and can state his name. Within 15 minutes he is Alert and Oriented ×4, asking 'Wow, I feel a little shaky. What happened?' Repeat glucose during the recovery reads 110 mg/dL; the skin turns pink, warm, and dry; and the heart rate settles to 88. Two protocol details matter here. First, reassessment is a named protocol step, not a courtesy: vitals, mental status, and — because his mentation had not yet normalized when it was drawn — the repeat glucose. Once his mental status has returned to normal, the protocol is equally explicit that further glucose checks are NOT required. Second, what glucagon spent cannot be spent twice: his hepatic glycogen stores are now partly emptied, and the nightly basal Lantus dose is still ahead of him — facts that belong in the disposition conversation coming next. Document what was found, what was given, and what changed.

John is fully recovered, mildly embarrassed, and hungry. Mary offers to make him a peanut butter and jelly sandwich, and he asks whether he really has to go to the hospital. MATC does not leave this to gut feel — it gives an 8-item checklist, release without transport only if ALL are true. Walk it literally: (i) repeat glucose 110 is greater than 80 — pass. (ii) He takes insulin and metformin and does NOT take long-acting oral sulfonylureas — read the words carefully: the criterion excludes glipizide/glyburide-class agents, not long-acting insulin, so Lantus PASSES this criterion as written. (iii) Normal mental status, no focal deficits — pass. (iv) He can promptly obtain and will eat a carbohydrate meal — the PB&J, pass. (vi) A reliable adult (Mary) will stay with him — pass. (vii) No major co-morbid symptoms — no chest pain, dyspnea, seizure, or intoxication — pass. (viii) Clear cause identified — a missed meal, the protocol's own example — pass. Everything therefore hinges on (v): the patient must REFUSE transport AND the EMS clinicians must AGREE transport is not indicated. Offer transport first. If he accepts, he goes. If he refuses, the protocol permits release — and criterion (v) is exactly where the crew's judgment belongs: this recovery was bought with glucagon, which spent hepatic glycogen stores he cannot instantly replace, and the nightly basal Lantus dose is still ahead of him. The source curriculum is explicit that he should be strongly encouraged to accept transport because the glucagon may wear off. Whatever is decided, the performance measure is blunt: if released at scene, the criteria are documented.

Improper Care Branch
BGL never checked
Trigger: Blood glucose was never checked and no glucose was given (or oral glucose was inappropriately attempted). Approximately 10 minutes after initial contact the untreated hypoglycemia deepens: he stops mumbling, responds only to painful stimuli with a groan, respirations slow to 10 and shallow, and he is at high risk for seizure.
The crew never put a glucometer on him — or worse, tried to force oral glucose into a confused man who cannot swallow. Ten minutes later the mumbling has stopped, and not because he improved: he now responds only to a sternal rub with a groan. The untreated sugar has fallen to 30 mg/dL, his respirations have slowed to 10 and gone shallow, his pressure is drifting down at 102/64, and the pulse is 124 and thready. He is at high risk for a seizure, and the quiet patient is the emergency the loud one never was. The rescue now runs through the MATC airway ladder BEFORE the sugar — and every rung of it is BLS: open and maintain the airway with positioning, suction ready, an adjunct sized to his reflexes (NPA while a gag persists, OPA if it is gone), oxygen titrated to 94–98%, and BVM support of his inadequate spontaneous respirations at the protocol's adult rate. Only with the airway and ventilation held does the crew get the glucose it owed him ten minutes ago: check the BGL (30 mg/dL) and give glucagon 1 mg IM/IN — oral glucose is absolutely contraindicated at this level of consciousness, and IV dextrose is out of reach at BLS scope. This patient is critical and transports emergently; dextrose remains the definitive therapy he will get from an intercepting ALS unit or the emergency department. The debrief writes itself: 'not acting right' = check the sugar, and a BGL is a vital sign in every AMS patient.
