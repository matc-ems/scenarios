Initial Contact
As the crew approaches, Frank waves a hand dismissively: 'I don't know why she bothered you folks. I'm fine. It's just a bum ankle. I don't need a hospital.' The trap on this call is taking him at his word. He is alert and oriented ×4, his SBP is over 90, and he will happily sign whatever ends the visit — but the assessment tells a different story. A local infection (the red, hot, edematous leg with a crusted rose-bush abrasion at the ankle) plus systemic signs (temp 101.8 °F, HR 116, RR 22, flushed hot dry skin) is cellulitis progressing to sepsis. MATC's Shock guideline needs only ONE sign of poor perfusion for inclusion, and he has several: respiratory rate greater than 20, tachycardia out of proportion to temperature, and flushed/ruddy skin. His SBP of 108 is not reassurance — it is compensation, and in adults the tachycardia phase of compensated shock can quietly persist for hours before it collapses in minutes. The clinical work in this phase — full vitals WITH a temperature, exposing and comparing both legs, taking Sarah's change-from-baseline report seriously — is not busywork alongside the persuasion problem. It IS the persuasion problem: every protocol-directed finding the crew gathers becomes a piece of evidence they can put in front of a proud man who lacks insight into how sick he is. No hands-on treatment can begin until he consents. The conversation is the first intervention, and it is built out of the assessment.

Improper Care Branch
Decompensated septic shock
Trigger: After a prolonged standoff, Frank decompensates — new confusion and disorientation, gray-mottled color change, SBP 78 mmHg. Decision-making capacity is lost; the Altered Mental Status inclusion criterion is met and treatment proceeds under implied consent.
The compensation ran out in the middle of the argument. Frank's color grayed, his sentences stopped tracking, and he slumped sideways in the recliner — GCS 12, SBP 78/44, HR 132, RR 26 and shallow, SpO2 91%. This is the Shock guideline's warning made flesh: hypotension is uncompensated shock, and it can progress to cardiopulmonary failure within minutes. The legal landscape changed with the physiology — a man who is confused and disoriented no longer has decision-making capacity, the Altered Mental Status guideline's inclusion criterion is met, and care proceeds under implied consent without the refusal he can no longer maintain. The rescue is BLS discipline done fast: position him and keep the airway patent with suction at hand, oxygen up — 91% is below the protocol's 94–98% target — with the BVM out and ready the moment his breathing becomes inadequate, load without another word of debate, advance notification to the hospital, and the ALS intercept for the fluid resuscitation the protocol calls essential and this unit cannot start. The debrief writes itself: this is the same BLS care and transport the successful crew delivered in phase two, started twenty minutes later, on a patient who may not survive the difference. The persuasion was never a soft skill running alongside the medicine. It was the thing holding the door open between a septic man and his protocol.
