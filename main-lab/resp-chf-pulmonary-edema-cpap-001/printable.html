Initial Contact
The crew finds a 72-year-old woman tripoding on the edge of her bed in obvious, severe respiratory distress: RR 34 and labored, SpO2 86% on room air, one-to-two-word dyspnea, accessory muscle use, and skin that is pale, cool, and diaphoretic. Auscultation reveals coarse crackles (rales) throughout all lung fields, most prominent at the bases, with NO wheeze. Both ankles show 2+ pitting edema. Her radial pulse is rapid and irregularly irregular — her known A-Fib — at 118, with a BP of 188/102. She is alert and oriented but physically cannot give a history — the whole story lives with her husband: CHF, hypertension, A-Fib, a missed furosemide dose yesterday, and two nights of worsening orthopnea. This is the assessment fork the scenario is built on. MATC's Respiratory Distress guideline distinguishes bronchospastic etiology (wheezing, asthma/COPD history) from fluid-overload etiology (rales, peripheral edema, CHF and diuretic history) — and every finding on this patient lands in the fluid-overload column. That pattern routes her to CPAP, NOT to a nebulizer, and NOT to a non-rebreather alone. A non-rebreather raises FiO2 but does nothing about the fluid-filled alveoli; positive pressure is the mechanical fix, and the protocol says NIV should be administered for severe respiratory distress. A teaching point rides along: the missed 'water pill' explains the mechanism, but replacing it is not field therapy — the protocol notes pulmonary edema is more commonly a volume-distribution problem, and diuretics like furosemide provide no immediate benefit and can cause significant harm. For this crew the treatment IS positioning, oxygen, and CPAP, with early transport and hospital notification carrying the rest. The clock starts now — she is working hard, and patients this age do not sustain a respiratory rate of 34 for long.

CPAP applied
Trigger: CPAP assembled correctly, connected to high-flow oxygen, applied with a good mask seal, and the patient coached through the initial anxiety. PEEP titrated stepwise from 5 cm H2O.
The crew explains the mask to Mrs. Peters in a calm, confident voice — 'this is going to feel like sticking your head out a car window, but it will make breathing easier; breathe with it, not against it' — and holds it to her face before strapping it, letting her acclimate. Within a couple of minutes of a good seal her SpO2 begins to climb and the respiratory rate starts down. ↳ MATC: Airway Management (Includes RSI)
critical
CPAP with PEEP 5 cm H2O initial; increase 2–3 cm H2O stepwise to lowest effective level
PEEP — 5 cmH2O is generally an appropriate initial PEEP setting. Increase PEEP in stepwise fashion (2–3 cmH2O at a time) as necessary, allowing time for the patient to equilibrate with each change before further adjustments are made. The goal is to reach the lowest PEEP needed to adequately ventilate the patient. Higher PEEP results in greater negative hemodynamic impact. Generally, physician consultation should be considered for higher PEEP levels (greater than 10–15 cmH2O).

Improper Care Branch
CPAP delayed; NRB only
Trigger: CPAP was never applied, was significantly delayed, was replaced by a non-rebreather alone, or the seal was broken after initiation. Roughly 10 minutes from initial contact, the patient exhausts: respiratory effort weakens, breathing slows to 10/min and shallow, and she becomes responsive to verbal stimuli only with a moan — decompensated respiratory failure progressing toward respiratory arrest.
The crew either never recognized the CPAP indication, delayed it too long, settled for a non-rebreather, or broke the seal after starting — and about ten minutes into the call, fatigue wins. The non-rebreather raised her FiO2, but oxygen cannot cross alveoli that are full of fluid; the mechanical problem was never addressed. Her respiratory effort weakens as she exhausts, breathing slows to 10 shallow breaths a minute bordering on agonal, and her level of consciousness falls — she now responds to verbal stimuli only with a moan, slumping back on the bed, no longer able to hold the tripod position that was keeping her alive. SpO2 is 75% ON the non-rebreather, BP has climbed to 205/112, and the heart rate is 130 and thready. This is decompensated respiratory failure progressing to respiratory arrest, and the window for CPAP has closed: NIV needs a patient who is awake, can follow commands, and can maintain her own airway — she can do none of the three. The MATC Airway Management pathway now governs: two-person BVM with PEEP supporting her spontaneous respirations at 10–12 breaths per minute, head elevated up to 30 degrees, an NPA while her gag is intact, suction staged at the head, an SGA if BVM with adjuncts cannot maintain oxygenation, and rapid transport for airway stabilization — requesting an ALS intercept en route if one is available, though ventilation cannot wait for it. Restoring oxygenation and ventilation is the whole job; there is no medication fix on this truck, and there was never one that mattered more than positive pressure.

Expected Actions
critical
Recognize respiratory failure — two-person BVM with PEEP now, head elevated up to 30°
Use bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation in the setting of respiratory failure or arrest. Whenever possible, the patient's head should be elevated up to 30 degrees. Two-person, two-thumbs-up BVM ventilation is preferred. PEEP should be used with BVM: 5 cmH2O is generally an appropriate initial PEEP setting.
↳ MATC: Airway Management (Includes RSI)
critical
Support her spontaneous breaths; ventilate 10–12/min with just enough volume for chest rise
Support spontaneous respirations if the patient is hypoventilating. Rate: Adult: 10–12 breaths/minute. Tidal volume: Ventilate with just enough volume to see chest rise, approximately 6–8 mL/kg ideal body weight. Over-inflation (e.g., excessive tidal volume) and overventilation (e.g., excessive minute ventilation) are both undesirable and potentially harmful.
↳ MATC: Airway Management (Includes RSI)
important
Place an NPA (gag intact); OPA only if gag is lost; suction staged
Oropharyngeal airways (OPA) or nasopharyngeal airways (NPA) can be placed if needed to maintain a patent airway and make BVM ventilation more effective: OPA are used for patients without gag reflex. NPA are used for patients with gag reflex. Suction airway: for significantly contaminated airways, consider utilizing a suction assisted laryngeal airway decontamination (SALAD) technique.

Supraglottic airways (SGA): Consider the use of an appropriately sized SGA if BVM (with OPA/NPA) alone is not effective in maintaining oxygenation and/or ventilation.
↳ MATC: Airway Management (Includes RSI)
