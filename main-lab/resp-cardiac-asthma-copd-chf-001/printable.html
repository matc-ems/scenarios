Initial Contact
Everything about this patient's chart says COPD and asthma, and he is wheezing audibly. The trap is fully loaded: a real bronchospastic history, an inhaler on the nightstand, and a sound in the chest that every student associates with albuterol. But this is CARDIAC ASTHMA — wheeze generated by fluid in the interstitium, not by constricted smooth muscle — and MATC gives the crew a clean discriminator table for exactly this moment. Fluid-overload etiology is suggested by jugular venous distention, rales, peripheral edema, and a history of CHF/diuretic therapy/hypertension. He has ALL FOUR. Bronchospastic etiology is suggested by wheezing, a 'shark fin' capnograph or prolonged expiratory phase, and a history of asthma/COPD. He has the wheeze and the history — but his capnograph is a NORMAL square waveform with no shark fin, and his EtCO2 is LOW at 32, which fits MATC's pearl that CHF tends to run lower EtCO2 than COPD. The exam beats the chart. Add the four albuterol puffs that did nothing, the PND, the orthopnea, the six-pound weight gain, and the skipped diuretic, and the picture is acute decompensated heart failure. The treatment is oxygenation and CPAP first, then HIGH-dose nitroglycerin — and, before a single nitrate touches this 68-year-old man, a direct question about phosphodiesterase inhibitors.

The crew got the diagnosis right and the safety check wrong. They correctly identified cardiac pulmonary edema, correctly reached for high-dose nitroglycerin at a systolic of 196, and never asked the one question that would have stopped them. He takes tadalafil daily for his prostate — a fact his wife would have produced in ten seconds from the shoebox on the dresser, if anyone had asked. Nitrates and phosphodiesterase inhibitors share a final common pathway, and giving them together does not produce additive vasodilation — it produces synergistic, profound, and poorly reversible vasodilation. Within four minutes his systolic falls from 196 to 74 and he loses consciousness. There is no antidote in the drug box. The management is now supportive: lay off the nitrate, run fluid, and reach for a vasopressor in a patient whose lungs are already full of water — the worst possible resuscitation to be forced into. The lesson is that a correct diagnosis does not exempt you from the contraindication check, and 'he didn't mention it' is not a defense when the protocol tells you to ask.

↳ MATC: Respiratory Distress (includes Bronchospasm, Pulmonary Edema)
critical
CPAP for the edema — but watch that PEEP will further drop an already-collapsed preload
The goal is to reach the lowest PEEP needed to adequately ventilate the patient. Higher PEEP results in greater negative hemodynamic impact. Increased intrathoracic pressure can progressively decrease cardiac output and is most notable when PEEP is greater than 15 cmH2O. The higher the level of PEEP (over 5 cmH2O), the more likely the patient will experience a variety of adverse consequences, both ventilatory and hemodynamic.
↳ MATC: Airway Management (Includes RSI)
important
Document the PDE5 exposure and the timing; debrief the missed contraindication screen
Patient Safety Considerations: The use of nitrates should be avoided in any patient who has used a phosphodiesterase inhibitor within the past 48 hours. Examples are sildenafil, vardenafil and tadalafil, which are used for erectile dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension. Performance Measures: Documentation of blood pressure reassessment in patients receiving nitrates.
